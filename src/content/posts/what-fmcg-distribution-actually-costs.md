Ask what it costs to become a distributor and you'll usually be told a single figure — the monthly commitment, or the opening order. It's the easiest number to quote and the least useful one.

The commitment tells you what the company expects you to buy. It doesn't tell you what you need in the bank to keep buying it. Those are different numbers, and the gap between them is where most new distributorships quietly fail.

## Start with what the commitment actually is

Jingo distributorships start at **Rs. 3 million per month** and run up to **Rs. 3 crore per month** for a full sole distributorship. The figure scales with the territory a distributor commits to covering — it attaches to the area, not to a rank, which is why asking "what's your MOQ" without naming a territory gets you a range rather than an answer.

Orders are paid **100% in advance**, online, before dispatch. That is normal for a new distributor relationship in Pakistani FMCG and you should expect it from any company appointing you for the first time. It also means your working capital funds the entire chain below you.

That last sentence is the whole article, really. Everything below is what it means in practice.

## The money that doesn't come back for a while

You pay the company in advance. You do not get paid in advance.

Shopkeepers in general trade buy on credit, partially, irregularly, and in amounts they decide. A karyana owner takes two cartons, pays for one, settles the rest next week or the week after. You will be carrying that gap across every shop on your route, permanently, as a rolling balance that grows as your coverage grows.

Market credit is the distributor's own risk. That's stated plainly in the Jingo distributor agreement and it is standard across the industry — the company is not party to what you extend to the trade. If a shop closes owing you money, that is your loss.

So the real question isn't "can I afford one month's order." It's "can I afford one month's order while last month's money is still sitting in sixty shops."

Budget for that cycle, not for the invoice.

## Stock that sits still

A Jingo sole distributor is required to hold **fifteen days of stock** in his godown. A sub-distributor holds **seven**.

That is not a storage rule. It is capital converted into inventory so the market never goes dry, and it is one of the larger costs people leave out of their planning entirely. Fifteen days of a Rs. 3 crore monthly commitment is a meaningful amount of money in cartons on a floor, earning nothing until it moves.

The premises themselves are your own arrangement — the company doesn't specify a facility. But the stock obligation is real and it's contractual.

## What the company carries, and what it doesn't

Worth separating these, because the answer varies by company and the assumption is expensive.

Tall Nest Group pays freight from the company to the sole distributor's warehouse, and from the sole distributor on to the sub-distributor. Delivery from the sub-distributor to retail outlets is the sub-distributor's own cost — the daily shop-to-shop running is yours.

Banners, posters and point-of-sale material are supplied by the company at no charge. A distributor who wants to run additional marketing beyond that does so at his own cost.

Stock damaged in transit to your warehouse is the company's responsibility, reported on receipt. Stock damaged after it's in your godown — rodents, rain, storage conditions — is yours. Which means the godown you choose is a financial decision, not a logistics one.

Stock approaching expiry is replaced, provided you tell the company before the expiry date with enough notice to arrange the exchange. Telling them afterwards is too late.

Sales support comes from the company's own field structure — National and Regional Sales Managers working with the distributor directly, Regional, Territory and Area Sales Managers helping set up sub-distribution, and company Direct Sales Representatives working retail counters in your territory. That's real cost the company carries rather than something you build from zero.

## The parts nobody publishes a number for

Vehicle. Godown rent. Order bookers and delivery staff. Fuel. A security cheque and documentation at appointment.

You'll find articles online that put confident rupee figures against each of these and total them into a tidy budget. Treat those with suspicion. A route in Johar Town and a route covering three tehsils of Bahawalpur have almost nothing in common in vehicle cost, staffing or rent, and any single budget that claims to cover both is invented.

What's worth doing instead is building the figure from your own territory: how many shops you can realistically cover, how many visits a week that takes, how many people and vehicles that implies, and what credit those shops will expect. That calculation is specific to you and it's the one that actually predicts whether the business works.

## The honest version of the answer

If you have Rs. 5–10 lakh and you're being quoted a figure in crores, you are being offered the wrong tier — not refused. Ask whether sub-distribution is available in your area. The margin at that tier is higher, the commitment is a fraction, and the company you're talking to may already have a sole distributor appointed who needs exactly what you're offering.

And if a company quotes you a commitment without asking a single question about your territory, your coverage or your vehicle, be curious rather than flattered. The ones who care whether you succeed tend to ask first.
