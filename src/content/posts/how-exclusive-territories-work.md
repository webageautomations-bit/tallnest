Exclusivity is the most requested and least understood term in a distribution agreement. Distributors want it because it sounds like protection. Manufacturers grant it cautiously because it is, in effect, a monopoly over part of their own market.

Both are right. It's worth understanding what's actually being exchanged.

## What exclusivity actually means

An exclusive territory means the manufacturer agrees not to appoint another distributor for a defined area, and usually not to supply retailers there directly. Within that boundary, you are the only route to market.

What it does **not** mean, in most agreements:

- It rarely covers every channel. Modern trade, institutional sales and e-commerce are often carved out and handled centrally.
- It doesn't prevent product arriving from neighbouring territories. More on that below.
- It isn't permanent. Nearly every exclusivity clause is conditional on performance.

That last point is the one that catches people. Exclusivity is almost never granted outright. It is granted *against a commitment* — and the commitment is the actual substance of the deal.

## Why manufacturers grant it at all

From the manufacturer's side, exclusivity is expensive. It concentrates an entire region's sales in one partner's hands, which means their performance ceiling becomes your performance ceiling there. If they underperform, you can't easily route around them.

So why do it? Because the alternative is worse for the category.

When two distributors compete in one territory, they compete on the only lever they control: price. They undercut each other to win the same shops, trade margin erodes, and retailers learn that the brand's price is negotiable. Within a few months the product is unprofitable for everyone selling it, and shopkeepers stop treating it as a line worth stocking.

Exclusivity prevents that. It protects the distributor's margin, which protects their incentive to invest in coverage — hiring a salesperson, buying a delivery vehicle, extending credit to new outlets. Those investments only make sense if the person making them captures the return.

In short: a manufacturer grants exclusivity to buy *investment*, not loyalty.

## What it costs the distributor

Which is why exclusivity always comes attached to obligations. Typically some combination of:

- **A volume or value commitment** — a minimum monthly or annual offtake. This is the clearest expression of the trade: you get the territory, they get predictable demand.
- **Coverage requirements** — a target number of outlets serviced at a stated frequency. Volume alone can be met by selling heavily to a few wholesalers, which is not the same as distribution and usually damages the brand's pricing.
- **Reporting** — secondary sales data, outlet counts, stock positions.

If you're being offered exclusivity with no commitment attached, be curious rather than pleased. Either the manufacturer hasn't thought it through, or the exclusivity is softer than it sounds.

## The leakage problem

The most common source of friction in exclusive arrangements isn't the manufacturer breaking the agreement. It's product arriving from somewhere else.

A distributor in a neighbouring territory sells a large volume to a wholesaler, that wholesaler's customers include shops in your area, and suddenly your exclusive territory contains product you didn't supply — often at a price you can't match, because it was moved in bulk at a thinner margin.

No contract fully prevents this; goods move. What a serious manufacturer does is design against it: coverage requirements that discourage wholesale dumping, pricing structures that remove the arbitrage, and a willingness to act when a partner is systematically flooding a neighbour.

Worth asking directly before you sign: *what have you actually done the last time this happened?* The answer separates companies who have managed a distribution network from those currently learning.

## When exclusivity breaks down

Exclusive arrangements usually fail in one of three ways:

1. **The distributor can't fund the commitment.** Volume targets were agreed on optimistic forecasts; working capital runs short; orders slow; the manufacturer eventually reopens the territory.
2. **Coverage was never real.** Volume was hit through a handful of wholesalers rather than genuine retail distribution. The numbers looked fine until a competitor arrived and the shelf presence turned out not to exist.
3. **The manufacturer couldn't supply.** The distributor built demand and then couldn't fill it. This is the most damaging version, because it burns the distributor's credibility with retailers they spent months winning.

Each of these is predictable, and each is avoidable with honest conversations before signing rather than after.

## A fair arrangement, stated simply

An exclusive territory works when both sides are clear about what they're exchanging. The distributor commits capital, coverage and consistency. The manufacturer commits supply, protection and a price structure that makes the work worth doing. Both commit to saying so plainly when it isn't working.

Everything else is paperwork.
