Two people can both tell you they distribute the same brand in the same city and mean entirely different things. One signed with the manufacturer. The other signed with the first one.

That distinction decides your margin, whether you hold territory rights, how much capital you need, and who answers when stock doesn't arrive. Most people evaluating a distributorship in Pakistan never ask which tier they're being offered, and find out after the agreement is signed.

## Who you actually contract with

A sole distributor signs with the company. A sub-distributor signs with the sole distributor for his region — not with the company.

Almost everything else follows from that. Your payments go to the sole distributor, not the manufacturer. Your stock comes from his godown, not the factory. If the company revises its trade price or runs a scheme, it reaches you through him. When there's a dispute about short supply, you're having that conversation with another distributor, not with a company sales office.

This isn't a lesser arrangement. It's a different one, and for most people entering FMCG distribution it is the realistic one.

## The margin runs the opposite way to what people expect

At Tall Nest Group, a sub-distributor earns **11% on trade price**. A sole distributor earns **3% plus 1%**.

People assume the bigger player takes the bigger cut. It's the other way round, and the reason is worth understanding because it tells you what each tier is actually being paid for.

The sub-distributor is paid for legwork. He is the one visiting karyana shops, placing stock on counters that have never carried the brand, collecting from shopkeepers who pay in instalments, and rotating product before it ages. That work doesn't scale — it is a fixed number of shops a man and a vehicle can cover in a day. So it gets paid per unit, and it gets paid well.

The sole distributor is paid for volume and for carrying the territory. His 4% is small per carton but it applies to everything moving through the region, including everything his sub-distributors sell. He earns by aggregating, not by visiting shops.

Run the arithmetic on a five-rupee candy and this stops being abstract. Eleven per cent of a Rs. 5 pack is paisas. The sub-distributor's business only works if he is moving volume across a lot of counters, consistently. The sole distributor's 4% is a smaller slice of a much larger number.

Neither margin is generous in isolation. Both work on throughput.

## Territory

A sole distributor holds exclusive rights to his territory, with no overlap from another sole distributor. That exclusivity is granted against committed coverage — it is a trade, not a gift, and it exists because the company wants someone investing in an area on the understanding that the return comes back to them.

A sub-distributor holds no exclusive territory. He works an area under the sole distributor's appointment, and the sole distributor can appoint others.

If exclusivity matters to you, that's a reason to want the senior tier. If it doesn't — and for a man covering sixty shops on a fixed weekly route, it often genuinely doesn't — then paying for it makes little sense.

## Capital and stock

Jingo distributorships start at **Rs. 3 million per month** and scale to **Rs. 3 crore per month** for a full sole distributorship. The commitment is set against the coverage a distributor commits to, so the number attaches to the size of the territory, not to a rank.

The stock-holding obligations differ too. A sole distributor holds fifteen days of stock in his godown. A sub-distributor holds seven. That difference is not administrative — it is working capital sitting on a shelf, and it is one of the larger hidden costs of the senior tier.

Both tiers pay for orders 100% in advance.

## Freight, and where it stops

Tall Nest Group pays freight from the company to the sole distributor's warehouse, and from the sole distributor on to the sub-distributor. Delivery from the sub-distributor to retail outlets is the sub-distributor's own cost.

Worth being precise about that boundary, because "we cover freight" means different things at different companies and the last leg is usually the expensive one. Here the company carries two legs of three. The shop-to-shop delivery — the one that happens every day, on a motorcycle or a Ravi, across a whole route — sits with whoever is doing the visiting.

## Which should you want

Honestly: the one matching your capital and your appetite for risk, not the one with the better title.

A sub-distributorship gets you into the trade at a fraction of the commitment, with a margin that rewards the work you'll actually be doing, and without a territory obligation you may not be able to meet. The common failure in Pakistani FMCG is not a distributor who stayed too small. It is one who took an exclusive territory on optimistic volume forecasts, ran short of working capital by month four, and lost the area anyway.

A sole distributorship makes sense when you can genuinely cover the territory, when you have the capital to hold fifteen days of stock and extend credit to the trade beneath you, and when you intend to build a sub-distribution network rather than visit shops yourself.

The question to ask any company offering you a territory is simply which tier the number in front of you refers to — and whether the smaller one is available if it isn't.
