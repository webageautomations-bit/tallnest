A distributor evaluating confectionery for the first time usually comes from a category with fatter margins and asks the wrong question. Not "what percentage do I get" — that's answerable in one line — but what that percentage is worth on a product that retails for five rupees.

The answer is: almost nothing, per unit. Which is why the entire trade is built around volume and route density rather than markup, and why a distributor who thinks in percentages rather than cartons per day tends to struggle.

## The chain, with real numbers

At Tall Nest Group the margin structure is published rather than negotiated case by case:

- **Sub-distributor: 11% on trade price**
- **Sole distributor: 3% plus 1%**

Both are calculated on trade price — the price at which that tier buys, not the retail price the shopper pays.

Stock moves company to sole distributor, sole distributor to sub-distributor, sub-distributor to retailer. Each tier buys at its own price and the margin is the difference.

## Why the smaller player earns more

This surprises people, so it's worth sitting with.

The sub-distributor earns nearly three times what the sole distributor earns, per unit. Not because he negotiated better — because the two tiers are being paid for completely different work.

Shop-level distribution doesn't scale. One man with a motorcycle can visit a certain number of karyana shops in a day, and no amount of capital changes that number much. He is placing stock on counters that have never carried the brand, arguing for shelf space against four competitors, collecting partial payments, and rotating product before it ages. Every one of those is manual and repeated. That work gets paid per unit, and it gets paid at 11%.

The sole distributor isn't doing that. His 4% applies to everything moving through the territory — including everything his sub-distributors sell — so it compounds across a volume the sub-distributor will never personally touch. He is being paid to aggregate, to finance the chain beneath him, and to carry the territory.

Thin margin on large volume, or fat margin on the volume you can physically reach. Both are real businesses. They are not the same business.

## What the percentage is actually worth

Jingo candies and toffees retail at Rs. 5, in boxes of 50 pieces, 36 boxes to a carton. Liquid chocolates, lollipops and bubblegums sit at Rs. 10, nimkos at Rs. 10, 20 and 50.

Take 11% of a five-rupee pack. You are earning in paisas per unit. A sub-distributor's income is therefore a function of how many units cross his hands in a month, and almost nothing else — which means his real constraints are route efficiency, shop count, visit frequency and whether the product actually sells through once placed.

This is why experienced FMCG people ask about repeat orders rather than opening orders. A carton that sells out in a week and reorders is worth more than ten cartons sitting in a shop that over-committed.

## Margin is gross, and several things come out of it

The published percentage is not what you keep.

**Last-leg freight.** Tall Nest Group pays freight from the company to the sole distributor, and from the sole distributor on to the sub-distributor. Delivery from the sub-distributor to retail outlets is the sub-distributor's own cost — the daily running, fuel and vehicle wear across the route. That comes out of the 11%.

**Credit that doesn't come back.** Shops pay partially and late. Market credit is the distributor's own risk, not the company's. A proportion of what you extend will not return, and that proportion is a direct deduction from margin.

**Storage loss.** Stock damaged in transit to your warehouse is the company's responsibility. Stock damaged once it's in your godown — rodents, rain, damp — is yours. A bad godown can cost more than a bad month of sales.

**Expiry handled late.** Stock approaching expiry is replaced provided you notify the company before the expiry date with enough notice to arrange the exchange. Miss that window and the write-off is yours.

**Staff.** Order bookers and delivery hands are paid out of the same margin.

Run those deductions and the number that reaches you is materially below the headline. That's true at every company in the category, and anyone presenting the gross figure as take-home is either inexperienced or hoping you are.

## What the company carrying costs actually means

Some costs sit upstream, and they are worth pricing when you compare offers.

Freight on two of the three legs is borne by Tall Nest Group. Banners, posters and point-of-sale material are supplied at no charge. Field support comes from the company's own structure — National and Regional Sales Managers with the distributor, Regional, Territory and Area Sales Managers for sub-distribution setup, and company Direct Sales Representatives working retail counters in the territory.

None of that appears in the margin percentage, and all of it is real money. A slightly higher percentage from a company that charges freight both ways, supplies no POS material and gives you no field support is a worse deal than it looks on paper. Compare the whole structure, not the headline number.

## The thing to be suspicious of

Anyone offering unusually generous margins on a five-rupee confectionery product.

The economics of the category are tight by construction: the retail price is fixed low because the buyer is a child with pocket money, and every link from manufacturer to shopkeeper has to make money within that five rupees or the chain breaks. A company quoting margins well above the category either has a cost structure nobody else has, or has not thought about whether it can sustain supply at that price.

Thin and reliable beats generous and intermittent. The distributors who last in confectionery are the ones who worked out early that this is a logistics business wearing a sales business's clothes.
