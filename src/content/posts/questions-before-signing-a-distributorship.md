Taking on a distributorship is a capital commitment dressed up as an opportunity. You will pay for stock before you sell it, extend credit to retailers, and tie up warehouse space and a sales team in one brand's product. That can be an excellent business. It can also be an expensive education.

The difference usually comes down to what you asked before signing.

Below are the questions worth putting to any manufacturer offering you a territory. Several of them are uncomfortable. How a manufacturer handles the uncomfortable ones tells you more than the answers themselves.

## 1. What exactly am I being given?

"Exclusive distributorship for your area" can mean several different things. Pin it down:

- Which specific areas, by name and boundary — not "Lahore," but which towns, which neighbourhoods, which routes.
- Does exclusivity cover all channels, or only general trade? Many manufacturers keep modern trade, institutional sales or e-commerce for themselves. That may be entirely reasonable — but you should know before you build a forecast that assumes otherwise.
- Is it exclusive for the full range or for specific SKUs?

Get the answer in writing. A territory described verbally has a way of shrinking.

## 2. What is the real coverage expectation?

Exclusivity is never free. Somewhere in the agreement there will be an expectation about how many outlets you cover and how often, or a volume commitment that amounts to the same thing.

Ask what it actually is, in numbers. Then ask what happens if you miss it — is there a grace period, a review, or does the territory simply open up to someone else? A manufacturer who says "we'll be reasonable" without defining it is leaving themselves room you won't like later.

## 3. How does the money actually work?

Margin is the headline number and the least interesting one. The questions that decide whether this works are:

- **Payment terms.** Advance, on delivery, or credit? Most manufacturers want advance from new partners, which is normal — but it means your working capital funds the whole chain.
- **How much cash will be in the market?** Model it: if you extend a week's credit across your route, what's the standing balance in other people's shops? That number, not your margin, determines how fast you can grow.
- **Who absorbs damage and expiry?** Confectionery in a hot climate will produce some. Find out whose problem it is before it happens.
- **Are there price-protection terms** if the manufacturer cuts trade price while you're holding stock bought at the old one?

## 4. What happens when something doesn't sell?

Every range has lines that move and lines that don't, and it varies by territory. Ask directly: if a particular SKU is dead in my area, can I stop ordering it? Is there a returns or exchange mechanism, or am I holding it?

The honest answer from most manufacturers is "limited or none" — and that's acceptable, if said plainly. What should worry you is a vague reassurance, because that usually means the policy will be invented later, in their favour.

## 5. What support genuinely exists?

Separate what's promised from what's funded.

- Is there a trade scheme, and is it in writing with dates?
- Are there point-of-sale materials, and who pays for them?
- Does the manufacturer run consumer marketing that creates pull in your area, or are you the entire demand-generation effort?
- Is there a field team, or is "in-house sales force" a phrase on a website?

Pull matters enormously for impulse confectionery. A brand kids ask for by name sells itself off the counter. One nobody has heard of has to be sold in, outlet by outlet, by you.

## 6. How reliable is supply?

A distributor's worst position is having built demand and then being unable to fill it. Ask about lead times, about whether they've had stock-outs in the last year, and about manufacturing capacity relative to how many distributors they're appointing.

If a manufacturer is signing up territories faster than they can supply them, you'll find out at exactly the wrong moment.

## 7. What does exit look like?

Nobody wants to discuss this at the start, which is precisely why it's worth raising. If the relationship ends — either side — what happens to stock you're holding? What notice applies? Is there a non-compete that affects what you can distribute afterwards?

A manufacturer who has thought this through has probably thought the rest through too.

---

## The meta-question

Beyond the specifics: notice how the answers arrive. Terms given straight, in numbers, without being chased, suggest a company that has made these decisions deliberately. Answers that shift, or that get vaguer the more precisely you ask, suggest terms that will keep shifting after you've committed capital.

You are being evaluated during this conversation. You should be evaluating too.
