Ask someone what a distributor does and you'll usually get some version of "they buy from the factory and sell to shops." That's true in the same way that "a restaurant cooks food" is true. It describes the transaction and misses the entire business.

A confectionery distributor is not a warehouse with a truck attached. The warehouse is the easy part. What a distributor actually sells to a manufacturer is **coverage** — a guarantee that product will appear, consistently, on a defined set of counters, and keep appearing after the first order's novelty wears off.

Everything else follows from that.

## The route is the asset

A distributor's real asset is not stock. It's a route list: the shops, their locations, the day each is visited, who at that shop makes the buying decision, and what they've bought before.

Building that list takes months. Maintaining it takes forever. Shops close, owners change, a street gets dug up for two months and three outlets stop ordering. A distributor who can tell you how many outlets they cover, how often, and how many of those took stock last month has something genuinely valuable. One who can only tell you their warehouse size does not.

This is also why manufacturers care so much about who holds a territory. They're not buying someone's ability to receive a shipment. They're buying access to a route that already works.

## Credit is the product nobody mentions

Here is the part that surprises people getting into distribution for the first time: a large share of the job is lending money.

Small retailers rarely pay cash on delivery for everything they take. Credit terms — a few days, a week, sometimes longer — are often what makes a shopkeeper willing to try a new line at all. The distributor carries that gap. They have paid the manufacturer, usually in advance, and will collect from dozens of small outlets over the following weeks.

That means working capital, not margin, is frequently the binding constraint on how fast a distributor can grow. Two distributors with identical margins and identical routes can end up in completely different places depending on how well they manage collection. The one who lets receivables drift quietly runs out of cash while appearing to do well on paper.

Anyone evaluating a distributorship should model this honestly before signing anything. The question is not only "what margin do I make" but "how much cash will be sitting in other people's shops at any given moment, and can I afford that?"

## Rotation, not just delivery

Confectionery is not furniture. It has a shelf life, it is sensitive to heat, and in much of Pakistan heat is not a seasonal problem. A distributor who drops stock and leaves is creating a problem that will arrive three months later as returns, complaints, or a shopkeeper who quietly stops reordering because the last batch went sticky.

Good distribution involves rotating stock on the shelf, watching what is moving slowly and intervening early, and being honest with the manufacturer when a line isn't selling. That last part matters more than it sounds. A distributor who reports accurately that a particular flavour is dead in their territory is far more useful than one who keeps ordering it to look good and then sits on it.

## Merchandising at the counter

In the kind of outlets where impulse confectionery sells — kiryana stores, tuck shops, roadside counters — shelf space is contested and tiny. Whether a product sits at eye level by the till or in a box behind the counter is often decided by whoever showed up, asked, and made it easy.

That's a distributor's job, not a marketing department's. Jars positioned, hangers filled, a counter display that hasn't been knocked over and abandoned. It is unglamorous and it is most of the difference between a product that sells and one that merely exists in the shop.

## Why manufacturers care who you are

All of which explains why a serious manufacturer is slow to hand out a territory, and why they ask questions that can feel intrusive. They are not being difficult. They are trying to work out whether you have the route, the working capital and the discipline to make coverage real — because if you don't, the cost lands on them. Lost shelf space is hard to win back, and a brand that appeared and then vanished from a shop is harder to reintroduce than one that never arrived.

The flip side is that this cuts both ways. A distributor should be asking the manufacturer equally direct questions: about supply consistency, about what marketing support genuinely exists, about what happens when stock doesn't move. A manufacturer who won't answer those plainly is telling you something.
